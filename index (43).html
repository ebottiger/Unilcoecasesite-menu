Top two misalignments

Two misalignments to focus on first

We didn’t pick these in advance. We tagged every point to the pyramid and these two fell out of the pattern.

1 · Strategy and behaviors

The strategy is customer-centric. The mindsets and behaviors aren’t, yet.

Strategies↔Mindset and behaviors

Why it comes first: the CRO raised it unprompted and the CHRO confirmed it with examples of their own, and it is the pyramid’s classic left-to-right gap. The new strategy and structure arrive, and the mindset shift gets left for later.

2 · Direction and measures

The direction isn’t backed by shared measures, accountability and ways of working.

Strategies↓Organizational shiftsInitiatives and workflows

Why it comes next: each function still works to its own number, which is the pyramid’s top-to-bottom gap: asking for a big shift while measures stay the same. Pay and targets are the open question.

My analysis

What we considered and set aside

  • The Purpose tier. The vision looks clear at the top, so the gap is making it real. We’ll keep testing it with Jamie.
  • Capability gaps. Real, but they show up inside both misalignments: leaders’ change skills in the first, functional skills in the second. Training alone won’t change what people are measured on.
  • Competition and the pace of innovation. Real, but Customer Success is Uniforce’s response to them. Phase 2 is about the new ways of working that response needs.
My analysis
How BTS could help

Initial ideas, and what we’d need to know to start

Uniforce brought BTS in for Phase 2 to help define new ways of working, and to help leaders understand how Customer Success changes their business model, teaming and measures of success. These first ideas are built around that, and will be refined after the Jamie interview. They run in parallel: shared measures tend to move behavior fastest, while leader capability takes longer.

For strategy and behaviors

  • A leadership alignment session that defines what customer-centric looks like in observable behaviors, by function, and turns it into new ways of working: how product, sales, marketing and support involve each other and the customer.
  • Targeted development for mid-level leaders: enterprise thinking and leading through change.
  • Customer-voice immersion, so product, sales and marketing hear churned-customer feedback directly.
  • Find and equip early champions who already model the shift.

To start, we’d need:

  • What “good” looks like by function.
  • Which leaders need support first.
  • Who is already modeling the shift.

For direction and measures

  • Co-design a shared customer scorecard (retention, NPS, usage, revenue per user) and cascade it by function.
  • Define how Customer Success works with sales, marketing, product and support (decision rights, handoffs and forums), so the new team changes how work gets done, not only who does it.
  • Build accountability routines around the scorecard.
  • Review how pay and targets line up with the shared goal.

To start, we’d need:

  • Where pay and targets point today.
  • Who is accountable when a customer leaves.
  • How Customer Success will be measured.
My analysisDraft ideas to refine with Uniforce, not a finished solution. Each comes with its rationale and what we’d need to learn first.

The numbers

Questions about the financials and the claims we built from them.

“Is ‘declining sales’ really the problem?”

Mostly it’s customers leaving and share slipping, not deals going unclosed.

Evidence

  • Churned customers rose 4.4% in two years (229,500 to 239,701). New customers rose 3.2% (225,000 to 232,190). Calculated
  • Retention fell from 49% to 46%, and the share of new customers fell from 9.0% to 8.0%. From Uniforce
  • Revenue follows the customer base almost one for one at a flat $250, so a shrinking base is a shrinking top line. Calculated

If pushed

Both executives describe declining sales, and they’re right about the symptom. We’d name the cause precisely so the fix is aimed at the right thing. My analysis

Not known yet

How the CRO defines “sales.” The CRO cites three quarters, and the financials are annual.

“How did you get to the value of one point of retention?”

About $1.1M a year, and it’s an estimate.

Evidence

  • One point on this year’s opening base of 443,890 is about 4,439 customers. At $250 each, that’s about $1.11M. Estimate
  • It assumes each saved customer pays for a full year. It is not a forecast.
  • For scale: this year’s increase in sales and marketing spend was about $0.45M. Calculated

Not known yet

When in the year customers leave, and whether saved customers would renew.

“Free cash flow is more than double operating profit. Does that make sense?”

It looks off to us, so we’d flag it and ask finance rather than build on it.

Evidence

  • Free cash flow is $113.5M against operating profit of $53.7M this year, about 2.1 times. From Uniforce Calculated
  • Rising days sales outstanding would normally tie cash up, not free it. My analysis

Not known yet

What sits in free cash flow: non-cash items, working capital, or a different definition.

“Why did days sales outstanding go from 50 to 62?”

We don’t know yet. We’d treat it as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

Evidence

  • Days sales outstanding went 50, then 56, then 62. From Uniforce
  • Possible causes: unhappy customers paying slowly, billing friction, or something unrelated. My analysis

How we’d find out

Ask finance whether late payers cluster among customers at risk of leaving.

“Margins are strong. Why not just invest more in product and marketing?”

Money isn’t the constraint. Where it goes, and what people are measured on, is.

Evidence

  • Operating margin is about 49% and debt-to-equity is 0.2. From Uniforce
  • Sales and marketing spend rose about 9% this year while the share of new customers fell. Calculated
  • One point of retention is worth more than twice that spend increase. Estimate
